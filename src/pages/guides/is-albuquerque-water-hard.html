Is Albuquerque water hard?

Usually, yes. But "hard" covers a wide range, the number at your tap depends on where your water comes from, and it isn't the same thing as unsafe. Here's how to make sense of it.

If you've lived here a while, you already know the signs: a white ring around the faucet base, glasses that come out of the dishwasher cloudy, a showerhead that sprays sideways after a year. Those are all calcium and magnesium carbonate, left behind when water evaporates or gets heated.

Most homes in the Albuquerque area get water that falls somewhere between moderately hard and very hard. The useful question isn't really "is it hard?" but "how hard is mine, and is it causing problems worth fixing?"

What "hard water" actually means

Hardness is a measure of dissolved calcium and magnesium. Labs and utilities usually report it as milligrams per liter (mg/L) of calcium carbonate, which is the same as parts per million (ppm). Water treatment companies tend to use grains per gallon (gpg). One grain per gallon equals 17.1 mg/L, so dividing a mg/L figure by 17.1 converts it.

The U.S. Geological Survey uses these general categories:

Very hardOver 180Over 10.5

You'll find other scales online with different cutoffs. They're mostly marketing charts. The USGS ranges above are the ones most labs and utilities line up with.

Why hardness varies around the metro

There's no single "Albuquerque water." What comes out of your tap depends on who supplies it and where they get it.

  • Albuquerque and much of Bernalillo County are served by the Albuquerque Bernalillo County Water Utility Authority (ABCWUA). It blends treated surface water from the San Juan–Chama Drinking Water Project with groundwater from the aquifer. In dry years the blend leans more on groundwater, so the mineral content at your tap isn't fixed from year to year.
  • Rio Rancho, Bernalillo, Los Lunas, Belen, Bosque Farms and Edgewood rely on groundwater pumped from their own wells. Groundwater picks up minerals as it moves through rock and sediment, which is why well-supplied systems in New Mexico tend to run hard.
  • Private wells, common in Corrales, Placitas, the East Mountains and the valley, can be very different from a well a mile away. East Mountain wells draw largely from limestone, which is essentially calcium carbonate.

That's also why your neighbor's test results don't tell you much about yours if you're on different water sources.

How to find your own number

1. Check your utility's annual water quality report

Every community water system publishes a Consumer Confidence Report each year. Hardness isn't a health-regulated contaminant, so some reports list it in a secondary or "other parameters" section and some leave it out. If yours doesn't show it, call the utility and ask. Our guide to reading your water quality report walks through where to look.

2. Use a test strip

Hardness strips from a hardware store cost a few dollars and get you into the right range. They're fine for "is this soft or very hard?" and not great for sizing equipment.

3. Get an on-site or lab test

A titration test at the tap gives a precise number. That's what our free in-home water test uses, along with readings for TDS, pH, chlorine and iron. If you're on a private well, hardness is only one of several things worth testing. See our well testing guide.

What your number means day to day

These are rough expectations, not hard rules. Water use, water heater type and how often you clean all play a part.

HardnessWhat you're likely to notice
Under 3.5 gpgVery little. Occasional spotting at most.
3.5–7 gpgWater spots on glass and dishes; slow buildup on faucet aerators.
7–10.5 gpgVisible crust on fixtures, scale inside kettles and water heaters, soap that doesn't lather well.
Over 10.5 gpgFast scale buildup, clogged showerheads, shorter life for water heaters and appliances, more frequent maintenance on tankless units.

Our dry air makes some of it more noticeable. Water evaporates quickly here, which leaves minerals behind faster on glass shower doors and dish racks.

Is hard water bad for you?

No. Hardness is not a health hazard, and the EPA doesn't set a health-based limit for it. Calcium and magnesium are minerals people also get from food. Hard water is a household problem: it's hard on plumbing, appliances and cleaning time, and some people find their skin and hair feel drier with it.

That distinction matters, because a softener is not a safety device. It doesn't remove arsenic, nitrate, lead or bacteria. If you're worried about what's in your water rather than how it behaves, start with a test, not a softener.

When a softener is worth it (and when it isn't)

A softener tends to pay off when:

  • Your water tests hard or very hard and you're seeing scale on fixtures or inside your water heater.
  • You have a tankless water heater. Scale builds up quickly in their small heat exchangers. We cover this in softeners and tankless heaters.
  • You're tired of the cleaning, or you're replacing showerheads and aerators regularly.

It may not be worth it when your water is only moderately hard and nothing bothers you, or when you rent and can't install one. In those cases, descaling the water heater on schedule and cleaning aerators now and then may be all you need.

Does Albuquerque add anything to make the water harder?

No. Hardness comes from minerals the water picks up naturally from rock and sediment. Utilities treat water to make it safe; they don't add hardness.

Will boiling water make it softer?

Boiling drives some calcium carbonate out of solution, which is the scale you see in a kettle. It doesn't help for a whole house and isn't a practical fix.

Is softened water okay to drink?

For most people, yes. A softener adds a small amount of sodium (or potassium, if you use potassium chloride) in proportion to how hard the water was. If you're on a sodium-restricted diet, ask your doctor, or drink from a reverse osmosis faucet, which removes most of it.
