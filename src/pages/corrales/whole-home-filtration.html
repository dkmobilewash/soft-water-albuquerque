Why carbon earns its place on a valley well

Every home in Corrales runs on a private well, and well water arrives unfinished. There's no treatment plant ahead of you settling out sediment or polishing the taste. Some homeowners chlorinate their own well after a repair or a bacteria result, and then they're living with the chlorine smell that follows. Catalytic carbon reduces chlorine taste and odor, sediment and many volatile organic compounds, which covers a lot of the day-to-day complaints we hear in the village.

Be clear about the limits, though. Carbon doesn't remove hardness, nitrate or arsenic. With septic systems sharing so many Corrales lots with wells, nitrate is worth a lab test, and if it needs reducing, that's a job for reverse osmosis rather than a whole-home filter.

What the free test checks first

At your sink we measure hardness, total dissolved solids, pH, chlorine, iron and manganese, and sulfur. Those numbers tell us whether plain catalytic carbon will do or whether your well needs specialty media for iron, manganese or a rotten-egg sulfur smell. For bacteria, nitrate and arsenic we help you set up lab testing, because those can't be judged on the spot.

Filtration questions from Corrales

I shock-chlorinate my well sometimes. Will carbon take care of the smell?

Catalytic carbon reduces chlorine taste and odor, so it helps with the lingering smell after routine use. Right after a heavy shock treatment, follow the flushing instructions you were given before relying on the filter.

Does a whole-home filter remove nitrate from septic systems nearby?

No. Carbon doesn't remove nitrate. Get a lab test first, and if nitrate needs reducing in your drinking water, a reverse osmosis system at the kitchen sink is the standard approach.

My well water smells like rotten eggs. Is that something you filter?

That smell often points to sulfur. Our on-site test checks for it, and if it's present, specialty media can be added to address it. We only recommend it once the test confirms it.
