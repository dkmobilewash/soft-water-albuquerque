Septic, wells and the nitrate question

Corrales has no village water system and, outside a limited STEP sewer along Corrales Road, most homes use septic. Put those together on a rural lot and you have the reason a state groundwater study looked at nitrate here. It found nitrate in some Corrales wells, linked to septic systems, at levels below the health standard in that study.

"Below the standard in a study" doesn't tell you about your well today. Nitrate is invisible and tasteless, and private wells aren't regulated like public systems, so a lab test is the only way to know. Arsenic, which occurs naturally in groundwater in parts of the Middle Rio Grande region, is worth including in the same test.

If either one needs reducing, reverse osmosis is the standard point-of-use approach. A softener won't do it and neither will carbon. RO forces water through a membrane that holds back dissolved solids, and it's the usual way to reduce nitrate, arsenic, fluoride and lead in the water you drink and cook with.

Corrales RO questions

Will reverse osmosis remove nitrate from my Corrales well water?

RO is the standard point-of-use method for reducing nitrate in drinking water. How well it performs depends on your levels and your water chemistry, so we confirm with testing before and after installation.

Should I treat the whole house for nitrate instead?

For most homes, treating the water you drink and cook with is the practical approach, and RO at the kitchen sink does that. Whole-home softeners and carbon filters don't remove nitrate.

Does RO make my well water safe if it has bacteria?

Don't rely on it for that. Bacteria in a well call for disinfection. We'll help you get a lab test and refer you for that work before recommending drinking water equipment.

How often should a Corrales well be retested once RO is in?

Keep testing the well itself on a regular schedule, often yearly for bacteria and nitrate. Conditions around a well can change, and the results tell you whether your RO is still matched to your water.
